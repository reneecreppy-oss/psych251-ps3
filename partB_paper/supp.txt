Extended model explanation

Our computational model requires that we compute p(b), p(t|b), and p(u|t,b,r). To model our
tasks, we used a uniform distribution for p(b) (i.e., p(b)=1/4 for each of the four possible
beliefs), and a uniform distribution over observable objects for p(t|b) (i.e., p(t|b)=1/3 whenever
target t is in belief b, and 0 otherwise).
         Finally, p(u|t,b,r) is calculated through a generative model that captures how speakers
generate utterances that uniquely identify their targets according to their beliefs and their
(unobservable) propensity to use redundant adjectives. To compute this probability, we assume
that the speaker uses a simple mental model of how a listener would identify the target under
different descriptions, in line with the Rational Speech Acts and the Inverse Planning
frameworks (1-6,27-28).
         The simple listener model returns a uniform probability distribution over potential targets
that match the utterance. For instance, if the speaker sees a blue triangle, a blue square, and a
green square, then the utterances `the triangle' and `the blue triangle' would both return a
probability of 1 for the triangle, and 0 for the two squares. By contrast, the utterance `the square'
would return a probability of 0.5 for each square, and 0 for the triangle. Using this simple listener
model, the speaker selects the shortest utterance that uniquely identifies the referent.

         This listener model returns a uniform distribution over potential targets that match the
utterance. For instance, if the speaker sees a blue triangle, a blue square, and a green square, then
the utterances `the triangle' and `the blue triangle' would both return a probability of 1 for the
triangle, and 0 for the two squares. By contrast, the utterance `the square' would return a
probability of 0.5 for each square, and 0 for the triangle. Using this simple listener model, the
speaker selects the shortest utterance that uniquely identifies the referent.
         Speakers, however, can accidentally be under- or overspecific. We thus include a small
probability that the speaker will produce an utterance that is insufficiently informative (the
Underspecification parameter), and a small probability that the speaker will include a redundant
modifier (the Overspecification parameter). Naturally, speakers can be under-informative for
many reasons, including distraction, accidents, and maliciousness and the Underspecification
parameter is intended to capture the overall probability of failing to introduce a necessary
adjective, independent of its reason. Similarly, the Overspecification parameter is intended to
capture multiple reasons for which speakers are over-informative. Redundant color adjectives,
for instance, are often used by speakers to help listeners identify the referent more efficiently
than would be otherwise possible (38).
         We estimate both parameters through participant judgments (with different parameters
for each adjective type; see Parameter estimation studies below). Formally, the Under-
specification parameter is the probability that the speaker will believe that a proposed utterance
is sufficiently informative, independent of the simple listener model's output. Similarly, the
Overspecification parameter is the probability that the speaker will use a modified expression
without evaluating if a simpler one would have sufficed.
         We treat the probability of under-specification as observable and constant across all
speakers. That is, we assume that listeners do not question that speakers are generally
cooperative (as told in the task instructions; see OSF site), but they nonetheless understand that
they can accidentally fail to specify the referent.
By contrast, we treat the probability of over-specification as unobservable and variable across
speakers. That is, we assume that listeners believe that different speakers may be more or less
likely to use adjectives redundantly and that each speaker's individual tendency to use redundant
adjectives must be inferred. Nonetheless, we assume that participants have prior beliefs about
how often people speak redundantly.

S2. Experiment 1 additional information

Stimuli

Displays were created by combining 4 shapes (circle, square, triangle and star) in 4 colors (red,
green, blue and yellow). We generated 4 possible conditions for each display by crossing the
presence or absence of a color adjective in the target description with the presence or absence of
a contrast shape in the display (Table S1). Each trial included 2 displays. Depending on whether
the position of the Target and/or the Contrast Shape overlapped across the two displays, we
distinguished 6 patterns of overlap (Table S2). The combination of 4 display conditions and 6
patterns of overlap resulted in 31 trial types, which were labeled by the condition of the display
on the left, the condition of the display on the right, and the overlap pattern (e.g., ‘ACN’ stood
for Ambiguous display on the left; Contrastive display on the right; No overlap). 3 trial types
were not included in the study because they were inconsistent with the assumptions of the task

(Table S3). Based on pilot data to estimate task duration, we randomly split the resulting 28 trial
types into two lists, each including 14 different trials (Table S4).

Procedure

Participants first read a brief tutorial and had to complete a small questionnaire to ensure they
read the instructions. Participants who got at least one question wrong were redirected to the
beginning of the tutorial and only participants who responded to all questions correctly were
allowed to participate in the study. See our OSF site for screenshots of the tutorial, inclusion
questionnaire, and test screen.

Subject-level results

At the subject level, participants showed an average correlation of 0.59 with belief inferences
and 0.93 with reference inferences against our ToM model (Figure S1). Against our lesioned
model, participants showed an average correlation of 0.35 with belief inferences and 0.93 on
reference inferences against the alternative model. The difference in subject-level correlations
was significantly different on belief inferences (t(59)=5.43; p<0.0001 by paired t-test) but no on
referent inferences (t(59)=0.38; p=0.71 by paired t-test).

     Qualitatively, 75.00% of participants (n=45 out of 60) correlated better with the ToM model
than with the lesioned model on belief inferences, and 50.00% of participants (n=3 out of 60)
correlated better with the ToM model than with the alternative model on reference inferences
(Figure S2).

Trial by trial comparisons

Trial by trial comparisons are shown in Tables S5-S6. To generate these trackpad visualizations
that compare model predictions against participant judgments, we standardized average
participant judgments, setting the lowest observed average estimate was to 0 and the maximum
set to 1 (formally we do this by subtracting the lowest obtained value from all average
judgments, and then dividing them by the revised highest value; see OSF site for code).

S3. Experiment 2 additional information

Stimuli

Please see Main text for stimuli design. The visual displays were randomly rotated for each
participant, such that the location of the referents and blind spot was counterbalanced across
participants.

Procedure

Participants first read a brief tutorial and had to complete a small questionnaire to ensure they
read the instructions. Participants who got at least one question wrong were redirected to the
beginning of the tutorial and only participants who responded to all questions correctly were

allowed to participate in the study. See our OSF site for screenshots of the tutorial, inclusion
questionnaire, and test screen.

Exclusion criteria

Because the first four trials unambiguously identified one of the four cells, only participants who
got at most one of the first four trials incorrect were included in the study. An answer was
considered correct if participants positioned the referent market on the correct quadrant. Our
study aimed to have n=30 participants per list. Due to randomization and exclusions the final
sample size varied by condition (Table S7).

Results without excluding participants

For completeness, we also present model results with the excluded participants. Fig. S3 shows
our key results without excluding the 23 participants who failed to identify the correct target two
or more times in the first four trials. When no excluding participants, our overall model
correlation decreased from 0.96 to 0.95 (Fig. S4).

Alternative model

In this study we did not consider our alternative model because it is unable to make different
predictions across conditions. This is shown in Fig. S5. Here, all predictions about the critical
trials are centered in 0.5. Note that the small variation is due to the sampling nature of our
inference algorithm. This lack of variability is because this model does not consider speaker’s
word choices. Consequently, it fails to update its estimate of speakers’ redundancy, or treat the
adjective in trial 4 contrastively. Because the model has zero variance on common ground and
redundancy information, it is not possible to compute correlations.

Subject-level correlations

Subject-level correlations are shown in Fig. S6. We include these analyses for completeness.
Note, however, that the subject-level data collected in Experiment 2 was highly impoverished.
Each participant provided only four redundancy estimates, making individual correlations
difficult to interpret. Similarly, participants only completed one common ground inference trial
and we therefore do not compute correlations for that case. Average correlation at the subject-
level was 0.24 (95% CI: 0.16-0.32) for redundancy estimates and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.88-0.92) for
reference estimates.

S4. Experiment 2 in-lab variant

Experiment 2 in-lab variant consists of a simple replication of the extreme conditions of
Experiment 2, with participants tested live rather than online.

        Participants were presented with five consecutive displays, one at a time, allowing
participants to learn how the agent speaks. Fig. S7 shows the two experimental conditions. In
both conditions, participants completed five sequential trials consisting of the same displays and

the same targets. Participants were told that the speaker could not see the same (unknown) cell in
all trials (the top-left one in Fig. S7, although this was randomly rotated across participants). As
in Experiment 1, participants were asked only to infer the referent in each trial, and to perform a
joint referent and blind spot inference in the final critical trial to test if they adjusted this
inference based on their speaker’s preferences. The top row of Fig. S7 shows the ‘succinct
speaker’ condition, where the speaker produced size adjectives only when necessary. On the first
display, the description “The square” reveals that the speaker is referring to the bottom-right cell,
and all participants positioned the referent in the trackpad to that corner accordingly. Similarly,
the next description “The circle” uniquely identifies the top-right corner. By this point, the blind
spot must be either the top-left or the bottom-right corner. The third and fourth displays then
provide indirect evidence that the top-left corner is the blind spot. On the third display, the
description “The small triangle” again uniquely specifies the bottom-left cell. However, the
modifier “small” suggests that the speaker can see the bottom-right cell and adjusted their
description accordingly. On the fourth display “the rectangle” is now ambiguous. Combined with
the fact that the speaker has previously talked about objects on the top-right corner (in the second
display), participants were overall able to infer that the speaker was referring to the rectangle on
the top-right cell. Finally, in the last and critical trial, the ambiguous description “The star” could
identify the top-left or bottom-right cells. Our model predicts that participants should infer that
the referent is in the bottom-right cell and that that the blind spot is in the top-left cell. This is
indeed what we observed with participants (Fig. S7a for distribution of referent identifications,
and Fig S7c for distribution of blind-spot inferences). The bottom row of Fig S7a shows the
‘verbose speaker’ condition, where the speaker constantly produced unnecessary size adjectives.
The trial sequence was presented in the same order, with the difference that the speaker always
modified the referent with a size adjective. Thus, if participant mental-state inferences are
adjusted by how the speaker uses redundant adjectives, the inferences licensed in the ‘succinct
speaker’ condition should no long be warranted after the evidence in the initial two trials that the
speaker is unnecessarily redundant. Thus, our model predicts that, in this condition, participants
should be unable to determine the referent or the blind spot in the final trial, as can be seen in
Fig. S7a and S7c. Consistent with the results of Experiments 1 and 2, we found a strong
correlation (r=0.94; 95% CI: 0.90-0.99) between our model predictions and participant
judgments. By contrast, the rule-based model showed a correlation of only 0.67 (95% CI: 0.48-
0.89), which was reliably lower than our main model’s (Difference: 0.29; 95% CI: 0.08-0.44).

Methods

60 Yale University undergraduates were recruited and randomly assigned to the reliable speaker
or the unreliable speaker conditions (n=31 unreliable condition; n=29 reliable condition).
Participants were provided a link and completed the task on their own time. Stimuli consisted of
5 displays of 4 geometrical shapes in each of the two conditions (see Fig. S7). The description of
the target was written above each display. The displays were presented one a time and remained
on the screen in subsequent trials to avoid memory load. Participants were told that their partner
in the game could only see 3 of the 4 shapes in each display, although he was unaware of this.
Their task was to (i) identify the target in each display following the partner's description and (ii)
figure out which quadrant the partner could not see. Participants were asked to click closer to the
corner of the display the more certain they were that that was the target. At the end of the 5 trials,
participants were asked to indicate in a separate display which quadrant was their partner's blind

spot. The reliable and unreliable conditions differed only on the specificity of the descriptions: in
the reliable condition, the partner used size adjectives contrastively (e.g., to distinguish the small
triangle from the big one), whereas in the unreliable condition, he used size adjectives
systematically. In doing so, the size contrast could not be used as a reliable clue about the shapes
that he could see on the grid. Participants were therefore able to figure out the blind spot in the
reliable condition, but not in the unreliable condition.

S5. Over- and under-specification priors for Experiment 1

The goal of Supplemental study 1 was to estimate the model’s prior probability that agents use
color words redundantly, or that speakers omit color words accidentally in color-variable
displays

Participants

50 participants from the US were recruited through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk framework.

Stimuli

Stimuli is available at our OSF site. Each stimulus consisted of a picture with either one or two
geometrical shapes (squares, circles, triangles, and stars). In total we had sixteen single-shape
images and four two-shape trials. All two-shape trials consisted of two shapes of the same kind
(e.g., two squares) that varied in color. All trials had a target surrounded by a box (i.e., in the
single-shape images the figure was surrounded by a box, and in the two-shape trials one of the
two shapes was surrounded by a box).

Procedure

Participants read a brief set of instructions and were asked to complete a short quiz to ensure
they understood the instructions (see OSF site). Participants then completed eight trials in
random order (all four two-figure trials; and a random subset of four one-figure trials). In each
trial participants were asked “what would the average person call the object in the box?” and
could select either the bare description (e.g., “triangle”) or a color-modified description (e.g.,
“green triangle”). For simplicity the bare description always appeared on the left side and the
modified description on the right side. In the trials with two figures, the position of the target
referent (left or right) was counterbalanced.

Analysis

        Under-specification. In our model, the probability of underspecification is a fixed
parameter that is not inferred. We estimated this parameter by calculating the average number of
times that participants selected the bare description in the trials with two shapes that could only
be disambiguated by color (4 trials per participant), and we then calculate the average across
participants. Average participant endorsement of an under-specific description happened 5.5% of
the time.

        Over-specification. In our model, speakers’ amount of over-specification is unobservable
and can be inferred. We set this prior distribution by fitting a Beta distribution through maximum
likelihood (see OSF site for analyses) to the proportion of times that each participant endorsed an
over-specific expression in the single shape trials (4 trials per participant). This resulted in
parameters alpha = 0.39 and beta = 0.32. See Fig. S8.

S6. Over- and under-specification priors for Experiment 2

The goal of Supplemental study 2 was to estimate the model’s prior probability that agents use
size words redundantly, or that speakers omit size words accidentally in size-variable displays.

Participants

50 participants from the US were recruited through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk framework.

Stimuli

Stimuli is available at our OSF site. Each stimulus consisted of a picture of either one blue
geometrical shape (squares, circles, rectangles, stars, and triangles). Each stimulus contained
either a single shape (6 trials; 3 big shapes and 3 small shapes) or two shapes of different sizes (6
trials). All stimuli had a target surrounded by a box (i.e., in the single-shape images the figure
was surrounded by a box, and in the two-shape trials one of the two shapes was surrounded by a
box; n=3 surrounded the big shape and in n=3 surrounded the small shape).

Procedure

Participants read a brief set of instructions and were asked to complete a short quiz to ensure
they understood the instructions (see OSF site for details).

        Participants then completed all 12 trials in random order. In each trial participants were
asked “what would the average person call the object in the box?” and could select either the
bare description (e.g., “triangle”) or a color-modified description (e.g., “small triangle”). For
simplicity the bare description always appeared on the left side and the modified description on
the right side. In the trials with two figures, the position of the target referent (left or right) was
counterbalanced.

Analysis

        Under-specification and over-specification were estimated in the same way as the prior
estimation studies of Experiment 1. The under-specification parameter was set to 5.6% and the
over-specification prior distribution was set to a Beta distribution with parameters alpha=0.32,
beta = 1.12 (see OSF site for details). See Fig. S9.

S7. Over- and under-specification priors for Experiment 3

Supplemental study 3 was equivalent to Supplemental studies 1 and 2, with the difference that
we estimated the probability of modification and under-specification for all three types of
modification (size, color, and category).

Participants

50 participants from the US were recruited through Amazon’s Mechanical Turk framework.

Stimuli

Stimuli is available at our OSF site. Each stimulus consisted of a picture of either one or two
objects, one of which was always surrounded by a box.

Procedure

Participants read a brief set of instructions and were asked to complete a short quiz to ensure
they understood the instructions (see OSF site for details).

Participants then completed all 12 trials in random order. In each trial participants were asked
“what would the average person call the object in the box?” and could select either the bare
description (e.g., “triangle”) or a color-modified description (e.g., “small triangle”). For
simplicity the bare description always appeared on the left side and the modified description on
the right side. In the trials with two figures, the position of the target referent (left or right) was
counterbalanced.

Analysis

Under-specification and over-specification were estimated in the same way as the prior
estimation studies of Experiments 1 and 2. The under-specification parameter were set to 21.67%
for category, 4.67% for color, and 16.33% for size. Over-specification prior distributions were
set to Beta distributions with parameters alpha=0.29, beta = 0.22 for category; alpha=0.43, beta =
0.82 for color; and alpha=0.28, beta = 1.18 for size (see OSF site for details).
